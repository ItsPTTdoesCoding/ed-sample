Merging without losing the mission: what Water for Good's CEO says to plan for

Written from the public transcript of one episode of Nonprofit Hub Radio, for the team that makes the show. Drafted with AI; a machine checked every quoted line against the public transcript, and the table below shows each check. We never invent quotes or facts: a claim we could not trace to the episode is marked.

Many nonprofit leaders only consider a merger when an organization is running on fumes, the host says. On Nonprofit Hub Radio, host Megan Speer talked with Dave Levan, CEO of Water for Good, about a merger between two organizations that were both thriving. His account is practical: plan for the egos, the name, the donors and the repetition.

Start from strengths, and rule out the lighter options first

Levan says Water for Good and Lifewater had collaborated for years before anyone said the word merger. The talks began with sharing how each ran things, and the CEOs found "we had really complementary strengths." Then came a practical problem: "we're both about to invest in time and capital in things that the other one already has."

They did not jump to a merger. Levan says they weighed "a joint venture, a shared services scenario" and that each time there was still a gap. "And we didn't go immediately to merger."

Everyone gives something up, starting at the top

Levan is honest that this is hard: "it's a whole lot more fun changing other people than yourself." His rule for mergers is that "everybody has to give something up." For the two CEOs it began with the first conversation: "This new merged organization doesn't need two of us." Both had to accept that "to make this successful means one of us won't be CEO."

He says the same applied to staff and to identity. Lifewater, around for 47 years, gave up its name. People on the Water for Good side gave up processes and the feel of the organization, and gained a new CEO. He also explains why nonprofits find this harder than companies: without profit there is no single number to point to, so people hang on to program names, systems and processes.

Take the hardest conversations first

The process ran for years. Levan describes 2023 as evaluation and 2024 and 2025 as integration, with benefits expected from 2026 to 2028. In 2023 the CEOs talked first, then the senior teams, then each board and then both boards together. "We tried to come up with the hardest conversations first," he says, and the name change was one of them, discussed early so people had room to grieve before options were weighed.

The brand decision followed the strengths. The merged organization kept Lifewater's systems and processes and the Water for Good brand. A brand-new name was an option, but "you have to re-educate half of your donors," and in his view "Water for Good brand was a little bit stronger."

Plan for a dip in revenue

On money, Levan does not soften it: "you're probably gonna see your revenues drop off." Some donors wait for the dust to settle before deciding whether the organization is still the one they believed in. Looking back, he adds: "rarely, if ever, is there a merger where your revenues don't drop."

He puts the integration at about two years for his organization. He says each of the two organizations had a run rate of about six million dollars, and that the combined line dips and then climbs again. For some donors the pause was long: "We had some donors, great donors, who it was two years."

His point is to budget for the trough, because the goal is reach, not revenue. He says they served 1.3 million people in 2025 and want to serve 2.6 million a year by 2028.

You can't overcommunicate

The advice Levan comes back to most is repetition. He passes on a mentor's line: "About the time you can't stand to hear yourself say it again," it is "about the time the last person in your team is finally hearing it." In a merger, he says, that is true ten times over.

Asked what he wishes he had known at the start, he returned to this. A year and a half later, some donors asked, "did you guys merge?" His team used several channels, since "everybody listens differently": phone calls, fireside chats and podcast-style conversations. And the message that works is the vision: serve more people, more effectively.

If your organization is struggling

Host Megan Speer notes that nonprofits often raise merger only as a last resort. Levan agrees and gives it a name: "that's actually called a takeover." It is a different scenario, he says, and it moves faster. His advice is to start early: "enter into those conversations sooner than later." He adds that "doesn't get better later," and that early talks are low stakes: "You're not making any commitment at that level."

He says his team "couldn't find a lot of great nonprofit information on mergers," so they wrote two documents, an integration plan and a summary of the merger's benefits, and he offered to share them with leaders who are weighing a merger.
