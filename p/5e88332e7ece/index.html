Buy the pipes, build the brain: how Groupon's RevOps lead decides what to build

Written from the public transcript of one episode of Making Sense of Martech, for the team that makes the show. Drafted with AI; a machine checked every quoted line against the public transcript, and the table below shows each check. We never invent quotes or facts: a claim we could not trace to the episode is marked.

Every marketing team with an AI budget faces the same question: do we buy a tool or build our own? On a recent episode of Making Sense of Martech, Groupon's head of revenue operations gave a short, usable answer. His team bought one platform for messaging. It built almost everything else itself.

The host's introduction (00;00;29;24) sets up the stakes: each agent his team builds "has to prove it makes the team faster or it gets switched off."

The rule: would a competitor get the same edge?

His test for buying is one question. "If I buy something and my competitor buys the same thing tomorrow, do they get the same edge?" (00;05;20;19). If yes, there is nothing to gain from owning it. You are only ahead until the competitor buys it too.

Applied to Groupon, the split looks like this:

  • Buy: anything that is a commodity, regulated and needed at global scale. He names message delivery as the one thing he would never run in-house: email, push and SMS depend on deliverability, carrier relationships and compliance (00;02;16;01).
  • Build: "the business logic, the definitions, the context, the workflows" (00;04;36;18), the information an AI needs about how the company makes decisions.

Why buy the messaging platform at all

The host notes that Groupon's team had a ten-year-old stack and that the people who built its internal services were still in the room (00;08;06;25). He says the reason for the change was not mainly technical: "marketers shouldn't be opening a ticket to change a message or modify an asset." (00;08;32;13). He adds that the change management was "brutal" (same answer, 00;08;32;13). The host reports that campaign changes dropped from two weeks of engineering to under two hours (00;00;29;24); that figure comes from the host, not from the guest, and the guest was not asked to confirm it in the part I read [UNVERIFIED as a result].

What broke was not the technology

The team migrated messaging, built a data layer and set up agent operations at the same time. Asked what broke, his answer was about people and meaning, not software: "What broke was the definitions and the ownership." (00;19;51;08). Departments had their own goals and their own numbers, so deciding who owns each definition had to be settled first (00;20;21;17 paraphrase).

On data, the team moved everything into one warehouse (00;21;03;08) and added a customer data platform inside marketing so there was one source of truth (00;21;26;06).

Why it worked: the order of operations

The team did the messaging move, the data work and the agent work in parallel on purpose. "I don't believe that AI should be separated from the data," he says (00;19;04;13), and he admits "Our data was not structured" (00;19;04;13), so unifying it could not wait. The project had backing from the top of the company. "If the champions coming from the very top and this is going to be the top company project, everyone needs to build on top of it" (00;13;06;02). That gave the team speed and permission to switch off older processes.

What it cost

He is open about the downside of a top-down push. It produced "people complying on it before they believed in the process" (00;13;55;03). He describes the first reaction to any AI rollout as defense: "the first reaction from anyone indistinctly is protecting themselves in terms of defense" (00;10;03;25). For each project the team keeps one shared document that says who is responsible, who is accountable, who is supported, who is informed and who is consulted (00;18;00;08 to 00;18;22;26, paraphrase). Ownership is written down before the work starts.

A pilot program is not a strategy

His test for whether a company is serious about AI is a question he would ask an executive: what did you actually switch off? "In many cases they are just adding. So that's a pilot program, not really a strategy." (00;03;21;17)

He is blunt about vendors too. His hottest take: "most of the AI features are actually prompts with a price tag" (00;03;50;02). His advice is to put your effort into the data and the context, because models change over time.

Keep a person in the loop

Every agent has a human owner. The transcript has him saying the agents "have a human owner" (00;17;00;13) and that customer-facing work needs "always a human interaction for giving the green light" (00;07;50;04). He also leaves some things alone for now: features where the AI model makers are moving fast, because building now means rebuilding later (00;06;51;03, paraphrase).

Why the project is called Foundry

Groupon's AI program is named Foundry. He explains the name as "a metaphor of melting down the processes that we had before and recasting them" (00;11;21;19). The motto he says the team uses every day is "speed over comfort" (00;16;39;19).

Three things to take from this

  1. Ask the "same edge" question before buying.
  2. Own the definitions and the context. Rent the plumbing.
  3. Count what you switched off, not what you added.
